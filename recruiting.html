CMJ Recruiter Playbook
Section 01

Overview & Principles

Everything in this playbook is built on four ideas. If you only remember these, you'll already sound different from 90% of the recruiters this driver has talked to.

Principle 1

Lead like dispatch, not a recruiter. The word "recruiter" makes drivers tense up. The word "dispatch" sounds like work, like a peer, like someone who actually understands the job.

Principle 2

Be honest about the annoying parts. Live loads, regional triangle, percentage pay — tell them how it really is. Drivers have been lied to too many times to fall for a perfect picture.

Principle 3

Help him even if you can't hire him. If he's not a fit, find out what he wants and refer him out. You earn on the placement, he remembers you forever, and his buddies will call you.

Principle 4

Use exact numbers, not adjectives. "Good pay" disappears in five minutes. "$0.75 per mile equivalent on regional, $1,000 a day, $7,000-plus a week" sticks.

Why it matters

A driver who doesn't sign with you today will be looking again in three to twelve months. The recruiter he remembers is the one who told the truth and tried to help — not the one who tried to close him.

Section 02

Opening the Call

The first thirty seconds set the tone for everything. The goal is to sound calm, useful, and like a peer — not like someone reading off a script.

If he calls you (inbound)

Inbound greeting"This is dispatch — how can I help you?"

If you call him (outbound)

Outbound opener"Hey, this is dispatch. I work at dispatch and I'm following up on the application we got. I just need one small confirmation — we mainly work regional Amazon intermodal. Is that something you'd be up to?"

Branch the call based on his answer

1
If he says yes — "Great. If you've got a few minutes, I'd like to walk you through how we actually work so you have a clear picture of what you're applying for." Then move into The Operation.
2
If he says no — "No problem at all. Tell me what you are looking for — what kind of lanes, what kind of pay, what kind of home time. I might know somebody with trucks available that matches what you want." Then place him with a partner company and earn on the referral.
Why this opener works

You don't lead with company name or your own name. You lead with a job function. That signals you're not going to pitch — you're going to talk. The driver's defenses drop and the real conversation can start.

Section 03

The Operation

Once he says yes, this is the picture you paint. Keep it specific. Drivers can tell when a recruiter is repeating something they don't understand.

What we run

  • Rail yards, not ports. We don't run ports. We drop and pick containers from rail yards.
  • Amazon intermodal. Next stop after the rail yard is an Amazon facility or an Amazon third-party partner.

Section 04

Pay — Company Driver

Percentage pay, not mileage. The math beats mileage on regional, but you have to be able to walk a driver through the numbers without flinching.

The pay math vs. mileage

Industry average mileage pay: ~$0.65 per mile

Our regional loads: often $3.00 per mile or higher

25% of $3.00 per mile = $0.75 per mile equivalent. That's $0.10 above the industry mileage average — and you're still in the first 90 days.

At 30% after 90 days = $0.90 per mile equivalent on a $3/mile load.

Selling the percentage"Mileage pay is great when you're running long haul because the miles add up. Regional is different — fewer miles, higher rate per mile. When you take 25% of a $3-per-mile load, that's 75 cents per mile in your pocket. Industry average mileage pay is around 65 cents. So you're already 10 cents above the national average, before you even hit the 90-day mark when it bumps to 30%."

The 90-day bump

The reason for the 90-day window: by then the driver knows the operation, the rail yards, the Amazon facilities, the lanes. He's faster, he produces more — and when he produces more, he earns more. That's the deal.

Amazon peak season

During Amazon sales periods (Prime Day, holiday push, etc.) the rates rise. Some of our drivers cross $3,000 a week take-home in those weeks. Don't promise this as the norm — use it as upside.

Pro tip

Don't quote $3K as the standard week. Quote it as "what's possible during peak, when the market is strong." Drivers respect ranges. They distrust single-number promises.

Section 08

Driver Requirements

Short, clear, and honest about why.

What we need from a driver
  • Minimum 2 years CDL driving experience.
  • No endorsements required.
  • Physical capability for repeated drop-and-hook work. Three to four hooks a day means cranking landing gear up and down. We look for drivers in the strength sweet spot.
Documents to collect
  • CDL
  • Medical card

That's all we need to start the process. Amazon runs their own verification after that. If we end up referring the driver to a partner carrier instead, our in-house safety team will run a background check and may request additional documents at that point.

How to phrase the experience & fitness ask"We want at least two years behind the wheel and no endorsements needed. The one thing I want to be straight about: it's three to four drop-and-hooks a day, which means real physical work on the landing gear. We want you comfortable doing it for the full week. That's the only reason we ask about that side of things."
Section 09

Objection Handling

Every objection is a question in disguise. Don't argue — answer the real question underneath. Tap any objection to open the response.

▾ "The percentage is too low."

The move

Ask "compared to what?" before defending. Half the time the driver is comparing to a number a buddy told him or a number he invented. Once you know the comparison, you can speak to it.

Script"Compared to what offer? I want to make sure we're looking at the same thing. Because here's the math: you're getting 25% before any of your expenses — and as a company driver, that means no truck payment, no insurance, no maintenance coming out of your pocket. 25% of a $3-per-mile load is $0.75 per mile equivalent. Industry mileage average is around $0.65. After 90 days you're at 30%, which is $0.90 per mile equivalent on the same load. So let's compare it to your other offer side by side."
▾ "I want mileage pay, not percentage."

The move

If he insists on mileage after the math, refer him out. You earn on the placement and he remembers you for being honest. But first, get him to do the math himself.

Script"Mileage pay makes total sense for long haul — you drive more miles, you make more. Regional is different. Tell you what: pull up your last good week of loads, total the gross, multiply by 25%, then divide by your miles for the week. If that number isn't higher than your current per-mile rate, you tell me and I'll send you to a mileage-pay carrier I trust. If it is higher, we keep talking. Deal?"
Why it works

Letting the driver do the math himself is stronger than you doing it for him. People believe their own arithmetic.

▾ "Your loads aren't dedicated — I want dedicated."
Script"I'll be straight with you — we're not dedicated, we're regional. And before you write that off, hear me out. Dedicated sounds good because it's the same lane every day, but here's what dedicated drivers don't talk about: when the shipper has a slow week, they sit. When the rate drops, they're stuck with it. When the lane gets boring, they're stuck with it. What we run is a tight regional triangle — Harrisburg, Baltimore, Carlisle, Hagerstown, Chambersburg — and we're picking the best-paying loads inside that triangle every day. Same general area, different loads, better money. If after this you still want true dedicated, no hard feelings — tell me what lane and I'll see if I know somebody running it."
▾ "I want to be home every night."

The move

Don't fight it. Surface the underlying need first — most "every night" is really "I want to see my family." Then offer the regional rotation as a possible answer.

Script (company driver)"Honestly, if home every night is the priority, this isn't the right fit and I'm not going to try to talk you into something that's going to blow up your home life. But before you go — what's driving the every-night need? If it's family time, our regional rotation might give you more quality time than a local job that runs you 14 hours a day. Standard is two weeks out, then home for a long weekend — but if a load happens to drop you near home during the stretch, you can grab an overnight. So you're not actually gone the whole two weeks. Worth talking about?"
Script (owner operator)"For O/Os we don't enforce home time. You're independent — you set the schedule. We just bring the loads and you decide when and where you run. So yes, that can work."
▾ "I've never run rail yards / I've never worked with Amazon."
Script"That's a fair question, and most drivers I talk to haven't. Here's the honest truth: rail yards look intimidating from the outside, but the actual work is the easiest part of trucking. Check in, hook the chassis, paperwork, leave. No shipper yelling at you, no live-load timer running. By week two you'll be doing it faster than guys who've been there five years. Amazon's even simpler — palletized, fast. Want me to walk you through what your first day looks like?"
▾ "I've been burned by recruiters before. I don't trust this."

The move

Don't defend. Validate, then prove it through what you do, not what you say.

Script"You should be skeptical. I would be. Most drivers I talk to have been promised one thing and handed something different — and that's exactly why I don't pitch the way most recruiters do. Here's how you can test me: I'm going to tell you the annoying parts of this job, not just the good parts. I'm going to give you exact numbers, not vague top-pay promises. And if at the end of this call you decide we're not the right fit, I'm going to ask what you actually want and either point you somewhere I know is solid, or tell you honestly that I don't have a good referral for it. I'd rather lose you on day one than have you quit on day thirty."
▾ "My buddy makes more at his company."
Script"That's possible — pay varies by lane, season, and how the driver runs. Tell me what he's making and how — mileage or percentage, what lanes, how many hours a week, what expenses come out. Half the time when we run those numbers side by side, the gap closes or flips. The other half, he genuinely has a better deal — and if that's the case, I'll tell you and not waste your time."
▾ "My wife / family doesn't want me on the road that long."
Script"That's the most important question in this whole conversation, and I respect you for bringing it up. If home time is the deal-breaker, this isn't the right fit and I'm not going to try to talk you into something that's going to blow up your marriage. But before we close the door — have you talked to her about how the regional rotation actually works? Standard is two weeks out, then a long weekend home. And during those two weeks, if a load drops you near home, you can grab an overnight without it counting against the rotation. So you're not actually gone two weeks straight. Talk it through with her, run the numbers together, call me back if it changes the picture."
▾ "What about detention, layover, and breakdown pay?"

The truth — we don't pay these separately

Detention pay, layover pay, and breakdown pay are all $0. Do not dodge this. The honest answer is the strongest answer.

Script"I'm going to be completely upfront. We don't have separate detention pay, layover pay, or breakdown pay. The reason is we're a percentage-pay shop — your 25%, or 30% after 90 days, of each load already covers the time you put in. Way to think about it: if a load pays $1,500 and you're at 25%, you're making $375 on it whether it takes you six hours or fourteen. Some drivers prefer that because they're in control — push harder, make more. Other drivers want guaranteed downtime pay, and that's a totally different model. If guaranteed sit-pay is the dealbreaker, tell me now and I'll point you to a carrier that has it. I'd rather tell you the truth today than have you find out later and feel misled."
Why this works

Owning the "no" builds more trust than dodging would. The driver who walks away over this wasn't going to last anyway.

▾ "How fast can I start? What does orientation look like?"

The good news — there's no classroom orientation

When he asks this, he's ready. The flow is short and the answer is a selling point: no week of unpaid classroom time.

Send me your CDL and medical card, I approve your application on my end, and then we submit to Amazon for their verification. Amazon's process is the bottleneck — that's where most of the wait sits, and it's out of our hands. Once they verify you, we book your flight into New Jersey, you pick up your truck at one of our local shops, and you're rolling. No classroom, no sitting around unpaid."

Script"To get the ball rolling on our side: CDL and medical card. That's what I need from you today. Amazon handles their own verification after that. If we end up referring you to a partner carrier instead, our in-house safety team will run a background check, and we may need a few additional documents at that point — but for now, CDL and medical card gets us started."
Section

Red Flags to Watch

Bad hires cost the company more than slow hires. When you see two or three red flags in the same conversation, escalate before moving forward.

Red flags in the application

Frequent job changes

More than three jobs in 12 months. Chronic pattern usually means the driver creates the problem.

Vague answers about why they left the last gig

"It didn't work out" or "personal reasons" without elaboration. If they keep dodging, the truth is probably worse.

Recent accidents, DUIs, or HOS violations

Anything in the last 12 months. Verify with PSP and MVR before going further.

Reluctance to provide MVR, PSP, or work history

Honest drivers don't push back. Hesitation here is almost always hiding something.

Expired CDL or medical card

Could be honest oversight. If they don't know the renewal date, that's another flag.

Conflicting addresses, multiple phone numbers

Inconsistencies are worth questioning.

Red flags during the call

"I'll take anything, just give me a chance"

Desperation is not enthusiasm. A driver who'll take anything will leave for anything too.

Asking about advance pay or cash advances before discussing work

Order of questions tells you what the driver is actually focused on.

Pressure to start without paperwork

"Just put me in a truck, I'll send documents later" is a hard no. Anything they're skipping is usually the disqualifier.

Aggressive or hostile behavior

If they can't be civil on a sales call, imagine them at a rail yard at 3am with a chassis problem.

Story doesn't match the application

If the phone story contradicts the application, pause. Honest people don't have memory gaps about their own work history.

Concerns about drug testing

"How long after I quit can I pass?" Hard pass. We don't gamble on 80,000 lbs.

Asks about bringing a non-driver passenger

This usually masks housing, custody, or relationship problems.

Yellow flags (worth a conversation, not a no)

  • Long employment gap with a reasonable explanation — medical, family caregiving.
  • One DUI more than five years ago — depends on context.
  • Older driver returning to trucking — fine if years and physical fit.
  • "I'm new to Amazon intermodal" — most drivers are.
When to escalate

If you see two or more red flags on a single call, pause politely and check with the owner before moving forward. The cost of replacing a bad driver is always higher than waiting for a good one.

Section 11

Things Not to Say

Common new-recruiter mistakes that kill trust on the call.

Don't

"We pay top-of-the-market." Vague claims read as marketing. Use actual numbers.

Don't

"Trust me on this one." Drivers have heard this from every recruiter who lied. Show the math instead.

Don't

"Let me put you on hold real quick." If you don't know an answer, say "I'll find out and call you back in 15 minutes" — then do it.

Don't

Talk over the driver. If he's talking, you're listening. Period.

Don't

Dodge the detention/breakdown/orientation pay question. Quote the number — even if it's zero.

Don't

Promise peak-season numbers ($3K/week) as the standard. Frame as upside.

Don't

Push when he says no. Get his preferences, refer him out, earn on the referral, keep the relationship alive.

Memorability & Sales Skills

A driver who doesn't sign with you today is still looking again in 3–12 months. The recruiter he calls back is the one who left a mark.

Already baked into how we work

1

2

3

4

Layer these on deliberately

Slow down. Use silence.

New recruiters race through their pitch. Pause after key points. Let the driver fill the silence — that's where you learn what actually matters to him.

Once at the open. Once when you're answering something important. Once at the close.

Ask the question nobody else asks

Better questions"What's the one thing your last company got wrong that you don't want repeated?"

Steering toward 2–3 week rotations (the right way)

Our standard rotation is 2 weeks driving, then home time, but our top earners stretch that to 3 weeks before coming home. The math heavily favors the longer stretch — every truck restart kills half a day on each end and you miss the best Tuesday-Wednesday load stacks. The goal isn't to trick a driver into committing to longer stretches. The goal is to anchor the 2-week baseline early and surface the 3-week upside so the driver can choose with the math in front of him.

Six techniques to surface the longer rotation organically

  1. Anchor with the rotation, not the weekend. Open with "our standard is two weeks driving, then a long weekend home." This frames the two-week stretch as the baseline.
  2. Ask about goals, not preferences. "What are you trying to accomplish in the next six months?" If he says "pay off my truck" — the 2- or 3-week rotation gets him there faster.
  3. Let the math do the work. "Drivers running two-week rotations clear about [$X] a month. Drivers stretching to three weeks clear about [$Y]. You pick."
  4. Use the peak-season frame. "Heading into Prime Day. Drivers crushing the peak are the ones doing 2 to 3 at a time."
  5. Use the first-90-days frame. "Most new drivers stay out 2 to 3 weeks for the first 90 days while the routes click."
  6. Lean on the equipment. "The truck is your second home — fridge, inverter, fully kitted. Two weeks goes by quick when the cab is comfortable."
Sample script — natural framing"Quick thing on home time — our standard is two weeks driving, then a long weekend home, then back out. Most of our top earners actually stretch that to three weeks. The math: every time you come home you lose half a day on the front end and half a day on the back end, plus you miss the best Tuesday-Wednesday load stacks. Drivers doing three weeks clear noticeably more than drivers doing two. What does your home situation look like?"
When not to push this

Some drivers genuinely need home more often. Watch for: young kids at home (under 2), spouse with health issues, recently divorced, single parent, unprompted family stress. If those signals appear, support weekly home — the cost of a quit-in-60-days driver is always higher than a few extra home days.

Take notes on the personal stuff

His daughter's graduation. His truck payment ending in November. Write it down. Next call: "How's your wife recovering?"

Plant the referral seed every call

Referral ask"Even if this isn't the right fit, if you know any drivers running regional out of the Northeast, send them my way and I'll treat them right."

Peak Season Playbook

Amazon's calendar drives our business. Knowing when the rates spike, when the volume slows, and what to do in each window separates okay recruiters from great ones.

January – February Slow

Post-holiday slowdown. Volume drops, rates soften. Focus on retention — call your active drivers to thank them, check in. Reactivate cold leads. Deep-train new recruiters. Build the referral pipeline for spring.

March – April Building

Spring uptick. Volume picks back up, hiring competition starts. Push for new hires ahead of summer. Drivers who join now are ready for Prime Day peak.

May – June Prep

Prime Day prep season. Amazon ramps inventory. Frame conversations around the upcoming peak — "if you start now, you'll be running peak rates by July."

July (Prime Day) Peak

Biggest mid-year window. Rates spike, hours pile up. This is the $3K-week moment. Push existing drivers hard. New applicants — close fast.

August – September Steady

Back-to-school + tax-free weekends keep volume up. Cool-down from July but still busy. Recover and resupply — onboard the drivers who applied in June but didn't start in time.

October Ramping

Halloween push + early holiday inventory positioning. Lock in drivers for the November/December run. Anyone who isn't ready by mid-October will miss the biggest weeks.

November (Black Friday / Cyber Monday) Mega Peak

The biggest weeks of the year. Rates often hit ceiling. All hands on deck: every existing driver maxed out, every new applicant fast-tracked. This is where reputations are made.

December (through ~22nd) Mega Peak

Christmas push. Same intensity as November through about the 22nd, then it tapers fast. Keep drivers happy with strong dispatch — peak season is also when drivers shop around. Bad service in December costs you in January.

Dec 24 – Dec 31 Slow

Holiday week slowdown. Some loads still move but volume drops. Send personal holiday messages to every active driver. Costs nothing, drives loyalty into January.

Scripts keyed to peak windows

Spring framing (Mar–June)"If you start in the next few weeks, you'll be fully onboarded and running by Prime Day. Last year drivers who hit that window were the ones crossing $3K weeks. The runway matters — apply now, you ride the peak. Apply in July, you miss it."
Peak urgency (during Prime Day, BFCM)"I'm not going to oversell this — but right now is the busiest week we've had in months. Rates are up, loads are stacking. If you've got your paperwork ready, I can have you in the truck this week and earning peak money. If you can't move that fast, no hard feelings, but the rates won't be this high in a month."
Slow-season retention call"Hey [name], it's [your name] — not calling for anything in particular, just wanted to check in. How's the truck running? How's home? Anything you need from us? Slow time of year for everyone — wanted to make sure you're set up for when it picks back up."
The big idea

Peak season is for closing. Slow season is for building. Most recruiters only call when they need a body in a seat — the great ones are calling year-round, which is why they always have a pipeline when the peak hits.

Sample Call — Start to Finish

A realistic anonymized inbound call. The margin notes explain what's happening psychologically at the key turns.

Setup: A driver named "Marcus" has called after seeing a job posting. 6 years CDL, currently runs OTR dry van out of Pennsylvania, unhappy with home time, burned out on the recruiter pitch.

Recruiter
This is dispatch — how can I help you?
Opening with "dispatch" instead of "recruiter" lowers his defenses.
Marcus
Hey — uh, yeah, I'm calling about the job posting. Regional Amazon thing?
Recruiter
Yeah, that's us. Appreciate you reaching out. Before I go through everything, one quick confirmation — we mainly run regional Amazon intermodal, MD and PA area. Is that something you're up to?
Qualifies him in 10 seconds.
Marcus
Yeah, I'm interested. I'm sick of OTR honestly. Tell me about it.
Recruiter
All right. Before I do — what's making you sick of OTR? I want to make sure I'm answering the right questions.
The question nobody else asks. Buys massive credibility.
Marcus
Honestly, just the time away. My wife and I had a kid last year. Plus the pay isn't what they said it was. My buddy says he's clearing more than me on regional.
Recruiter
Got it. Two real things — home time and pay. Let me walk through both. Where do you live?
Doesn't argue. Doesn't immediately pitch. Names the two things, then gathers info.
Marcus
Lancaster, PA.
Recruiter
Perfect. So home time — Lancaster's right in our region. Standard rotation is two weeks out, then a long weekend home, then back out. Most of our top earners stretch that to three weeks because the math favors it. But here's the in-region perk: if a load happens to drop you near Lancaster during the two-week stretch, you can run home, sleep in your own bed, see your kid for breakfast, hit the road in the morning. That doesn't reset the rotation — it's just a courtesy.
Anchors with two weeks as baseline and three weeks as upside. Layers the overnight-courtesy on top.
Marcus
Two weeks at a time? That's still a long stretch.
Recruiter
It is. Here's what most drivers don't realize until they try it — the two-week rhythm actually clears more than weekly home time, because every time you reset the truck you lose half a day on each end and miss the best Tuesday-Wednesday load stacks. Plus the overnights when loads drop near home break it up. Now — pay. What's your buddy clearing?
Addresses the pushback honestly. Then moves the call forward.
Marcus
He says like $1,800 to $2,200 a week take-home.
Recruiter
Okay. We do percentage pay, not mileage. 25% to start, 30% after 90 days. Average regional load pays around three bucks a mile. 25% of three dollars is 75 cents per mile in your pocket. Industry average mileage pay is around 65 cents — so you're already 10 cents above the average on day one, and you don't even have the 90-day bump yet.
Specific numbers. Concrete and memorable.
Marcus
Hmm. But I'd rather just have mileage pay so I know what I'm getting.
Recruiter
I get it. Tell you what — pull up your last good week. Total the gross. Multiply by 25%. Divide by your miles. If that number is higher than your current per-mile rate, we should keep talking. If lower, I'll send you to a mileage-pay carrier I trust. Deal?
Doesn't fight. Hands the math back. Offers the referral-out path.
Marcus
All right, that's fair. What about detention pay?
Recruiter
Going to be straight with you — we don't have separate detention pay. Same with layover or breakdown. The percentage already builds in the time. When a load pays $1,500 and you're getting 25%, you're making $375 on it whether it takes six hours or twelve. If guaranteed detention is a dealbreaker, tell me now and I'll point you to a carrier that has it.
Doesn't dodge. Owns the no. Names the tradeoff.
Marcus
Huh. Most recruiters dodge that. I appreciate you saying it.
Recruiter
Means a lot. So with all that said — interested in moving forward? What I'd need is your CDL and medical card. From there I run it through Amazon for their verification. Once they clear you, we book your flight to NJ, you pick up your truck, and you're rolling. No classroom orientation.
Honest close. Frames "no orientation" as a selling point.
Marcus
No orientation? That's actually huge. Yeah, send me whatever you need.
Recruiter
I'll text you right after we hang up. Text me back the CDL and medical card today if you can.
Specific next step, specific time.
Recruiter
One last thing, Marcus. If you know any other drivers running regional out of PA or MD who are tired of OTR, send them my way. I'll treat them right.
The referral seed. Plants it before hanging up.

What this call did right

  • Qualified in the first 30 seconds.
  • Asked the "what made you call" question instead of launching into pitch.
  • Used the driver's location specifically (Lancaster, PA).
  • Painted a picture instead of quoting policy.
  • Walked the pay math and offered to let the driver do the math himself.
  • Owned the "no" on detention pay.
  • Named the Amazon bottleneck proactively.
  • Planted the referral seed at the close.

Industry Glossary

Trucking has its own language. If a driver throws a term at you and you go silent, you lose credibility.

BOL
Bill of Lading. The paperwork that travels with a load.
Bobtail
Driving the truck without any trailer attached.
Broker
Middleman who matches loads to carriers but doesn't own trucks.
BFCM
Black Friday / Cyber Monday. One of the two biggest peak windows.
CDL
Commercial Driver's License. Class A required for tractor-trailer.
Chassis
The wheeled frame underneath a shipping container.
Consignee
The receiver of a load. Usually an Amazon facility for us.
Deadhead
Driving with an empty trailer between loads.
Detention
Time spent waiting at a shipper or consignee after the appointment. We don't pay separately.
DOT
Department of Transportation. The federal regulator.
DOT physical
The medical exam every CDL driver must pass. Usually good for 2 years.
Drop and hook
Pick up a pre-loaded trailer instead of waiting for a live load. Most of our work.
Dry van
Standard enclosed trailer, no temperature control.
ELD
Electronic Logging Device. Federally required. $50/week for O/Os.
FMCSA
Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration.
FTL / LTL
Full Truckload / Less Than Truckload. We run FTL.
Hazmat
Hazardous materials. Requires H endorsement. We don't require it.
HOS
Hours of Service. Federal rules: 11 hours driving, 14-hour workday, 70 hours per 8 days.
Intermodal
Freight that moves on more than one mode of transport. Rail + truck.
Layover
Overnight stay waiting on the next load. We don't pay separately.
Live load
Trailer is loaded while you wait. Amazon is palletized so live loads max ~2 hours.
MVR
Motor Vehicle Record. State DMV record showing tickets and license status.
OTR
Over the Road. Long-haul, away for weeks. We are not OTR — we're regional.
Owner Operator / O/O
A driver who owns their own truck.
Power only
Driver provides just the tractor; shipper provides the trailer/chassis.
PSP
Pre-Employment Screening Program. Federal database of crash and inspection history.
Rate con
Rate Confirmation. Document confirming the agreed pay for a load.
Reefer
Refrigerated trailer. We don't run reefer.
Settlement
Weekly pay statement showing gross, fees, and net.
Sleeper
Cab with a bunk for OTR. Our trucks are sleepers.
Tractor
The truck itself (without trailer).
W2 / 1099
W2 = company driver. 1099 = owner operator (no withholding).
Yard / Rail yard
The intermodal terminal where rail and truck meet. NSRR and CSXT are the two big operators in our region.
If you don't know a term

It's better to ask the driver than to fake it. "Honest answer — I'm not sure what you mean by that. Can you walk me through it?" Drivers respect that more than a bluff.
